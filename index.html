One Month, Two Shocks

What 547,271 US flights from January 2024 say about delays and cancellations

Author

Zhiyang Cheng

Published

November 2026

January 2024 looks like an ordinary winter month at first. But when I loaded every domestic flight into a MySQL database on AWS and asked it questions, two separate disruptions stood out: a wave of winter storms, and the grounding of one aircraft type that hit two airlines especially hard. This page tells that story. The 7 SQL questions page gives the exact queries and answers.

1. Two shocks hit the same month

Every cancelled flight in the data carries a reason code. Weather means the airline cancelled because of the weather. Carrier means the cause was within the airline’s control (crew, maintenance, aircraft). National Air System means air traffic control or airport operations. Plotting them by day shows two very different patterns.

  • Weather (orange) came in bursts. Storms on January 9 and again from January 12 to 16 drove cancellations to 2,962 on January 15 (Martin Luther King Jr. Day). The five days from Jan 12 to Jan 16 alone hold 46% of the whole month’s cancellations. The average departure delay that day was 51 minutes, compared with about 9 minutes on a calm day like January 5.
  • Carrier cancellations (blue) behaved like a plateau, not a storm. They averaged 16 per day from January 1 to 5. Overnight they jumped to about 335 per day and stayed there until January 27, and then they dropped. Weather doesn’t behave like that. A policy decision does.

2. One aircraft type, two airlines

On the evening of January 5, a door plug blew out of an Alaska Airlines Boeing 737 MAX 9 shortly after takeoff from Portland (Flight 1282). The next day the FAA grounded 171 MAX 9 jets with the same door plug design. Alaska (65 jets) and United (79 jets) flew most of them in the US. Alaska resumed MAX 9 flights on January 26 and United on January 27.

Of the 7,736 cancellations blamed on the airline itself, 6,895 (89%) were Alaska or United. During the grounding (January 6–27) those two airlines averaged about 300 carrier-caused cancellations a day. The other 13 airlines combined averaged 33 a day, up from 13 a day before it. Alaska cancelled 17.6% of all its January flights, roughly 1 in 6. Delta, for comparison, cancelled 0.6%.

The outside events line up with the database. The plateau starts on January 6, the day the grounding order was issued. United’s line falls on January 28, the day after its MAX 9s returned. The data alone can’t prove that a given flight was cancelled because of the grounding. But no other explanation fits two airlines, one date range and one cancellation code this well.

3. Delays and cancellations are different ways to fail

One of the assignment questions asks for each airline’s longest delay. That turns out to be a poor measure of service. American had the single worst delay of the month: one flight left 3,125 minutes (52 hours) late. Alaska’s worst delay was only 684 minutes, the second-shortest of any airline. Yet Alaska cancelled more flights than anyone. Putting the typical delay and the cancellation rate side by side shows the real trade-offs:

  • Alaska and United sit high on the chart. Their problem was cancelled flights, and only moderate delays.
  • American, Frontier and JetBlue sit far right. They cancelled few flights (1.6–2.2%) but were often late: American’s average departure delay was 25.5 minutes, the worst of any airline.
  • Delta sits in the best corner: the lowest cancellation rate (0.64%) and a below-average delay (14.8 minutes). Southwest, by far the largest airline (115,389 flights), kept its average delay to 14.0 minutes.

A single worst-case number (Problem 1) or a single average can’t show this. You need both measures, and you need to know how many flights sit behind each.

4. Watch the sample size: small airports produce extreme averages

Problem 4 asks which airport had the highest average departure delay. The answer is North Central West Virginia (CKB) in Clarksburg/Fairmont, at 86 minutes. But CKB had only 15 departures in the whole month, so a few badly delayed flights decide its average. The scatter shows that pattern for every airport:

Small airports, on the left, spread from 0 to 86 minutes. Big airports, on the right, cluster between about 10 and 35 minutes. Among the 86 airports with at least 1,000 departures, the worst was Des Moines (DSM), 35.3 minutes. Iowa had heavy snow and extreme cold in mid-January. The best were in California: San Jose (9.6), Long Beach (9.6) and Oakland (10.5). The same caution applies to Problem 5: Spirit’s “worst airport” is based on a single flight. See Problem 5.

5. The reason behind cancellations depends on whose hub it is

At most airports, weather was the most common reason for cancelling. That was true at 253 of the 296 airports that had any cancellations (counting ties), including Chicago O’Hare, Dallas/Fort Worth, Washington National and LaGuardia. But at Seattle (90% carrier-caused), San Francisco, Newark, Los Angeles and Houston, the airline was the main cause. These are Alaska’s and United’s hubs. The aircraft grounding shows up in the airport data too.

6. If you can choose when to fly

Delays build up during the day. Each late flight makes that aircraft’s next flight late too. Of flights scheduled to leave between 6 and 7 a.m., 9.9% left 15+ minutes late. For flights scheduled between 7 and 8 p.m. it was 32.1%, more than three times as many.

Problem 3 ranks Monday as the busiest day, but that’s mostly a calendar effect. January 2024 started on a Monday, so it had five Mondays, Tuesdays and Wednesdays (blue) and only four of every other day. Per day, Friday (18,526 flights) and Thursday (18,478) were busiest, and Saturday was clearly the quietest day (15,879). The Saturday dips show up as a weekly rhythm in the 3-day moving average (Problem 7).

What to take away

  1. Look at causes, not just totals. The month’s 20,389 cancellations came from two different events. Splitting them by reason code and date separated a weather story from an aircraft-safety story.
  2. Most airline-caused cancellations trace to one grounding. Alaska and United account for 89% of them, and the pattern starts and ends on the dates the MAX 9s left and returned to service.
  3. A worst case or an average needs its denominator. The worst-delay airport and several “worst airport per airline” answers rest on a handful of flights. Reporting the counts alongside averages keeps a dashboard honest.
  4. Fly early in the day if you can. Morning departures were about three times less likely to leave 15+ minutes late than evening ones.

How the numbers were produced: the January 2024 file from the Bureau of Transportation Statistics was copied to an AWS EC2 instance, loaded into MySQL on Amazon RDS with mysqlimport, joined with 6 lookup (dimension) tables, and analyzed with SQL (window functions and CTEs). Details and data-cleaning decisions are on the Data & methods page.
